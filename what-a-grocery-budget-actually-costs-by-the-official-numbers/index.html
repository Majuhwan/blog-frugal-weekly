What a Grocery Budget Actually Costs, by the Official USDA Numbers

How the USDA's official Thrifty Food Plan prices out a basic grocery budget by age and household size, with the real monthly figures shown.

Frugal WeeklyOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

When you're trying to set a grocery budget, it helps to have a real, government-published reference point instead of guessing. The U.S. Department of Agriculture publishes exactly that: four official food plans (Thrifty, Low-Cost, Moderate-Cost, and Liberal) that price out a nutritious diet prepared entirely at home, broken down by age and sex, and updated every month using the Consumer Price Index.

What the Thrifty Food Plan actually measures

The Thrifty Food Plan is the lowest of the four cost levels and is also the basis used to calculate maximum SNAP (food stamp) benefits. It assumes all meals and snacks are prepared at home, no restaurant spending included, using a nutritionally balanced set of foods and quantities the USDA has specified down to the food category.

As of the August 2026 report, the official Thrifty Food Plan weekly and monthly costs per person looked like this for a reference adult:

Age-sex group Weekly cost Monthly cost
Female, 20-50 years $58.50 $253.70
Male, 20-50 years $73.60 $318.90
Child, 6-8 years $48.30 $209.20
Child, 9-11 years $55.80 $241.90

The USDA's own reference household, defined as a male and female aged 20-50 plus two children aged 6-8 and 9-11, had an official combined monthly Thrifty Food Plan cost of $1,023.70 as of that same report. That is a four-person household, eating at the most economical of the four official plans, with every meal made at home.

The adjustment rule for different household sizes

The USDA Food Plans publication includes a specific adjustment table for households that aren't exactly four people, because feeding fewer or more people doesn't scale perfectly linearly (a one-person household still buys some ingredients in quantities designed for more servings). The official adjustments are:

  • 1-person household: add 20%
  • 2-person household: add 10%
  • 3-person household: add 5%
  • 4-person household: no adjustment
  • 5- or 6-person household: subtract 5%
  • 7-or-more-person household: subtract 10%

So a single adult living alone on the Thrifty Food Plan doesn't just use their own age-sex row; the USDA says to add 20% to account for the inefficiency of cooking and buying for one. For a 20-50 year old woman eating alone, that's $253.70 plus 20%, or about $304.44 a month, not the unadjusted $253.70 the single-person row might suggest at first glance.

Three other cost levels, for comparison

The Thrifty Food Plan is the cheapest of four official USDA food plans, not the only one. The Low-Cost, Moderate-Cost, and Liberal plans represent the same nutritional adequacy at progressively higher budgets, using more variety, more convenience foods, and less emphasis on the cheapest options in each category. For the same August 2026 period, a 20-50 year old woman's monthly cost ranged from the Thrifty Plan's $253.70 up through the Moderate-Cost Plan's roughly $322 to the Liberal Plan's roughly $411, depending on which USDA table you check. This spread is useful context: if your actual grocery bill is well above the Thrifty Plan figure for your household, you are likely already spending at a Moderate or Liberal level, which is not a problem by itself, but it is useful to know where your current spending sits on an official scale rather than guessing.

Why this is a useful frugal living benchmark

A lot of grocery-budgeting advice online throws out round numbers like "$50 a week per person" without any sourcing. The USDA figures are different: they're published monthly, broken down by exact age and sex categories, and adjusted using real-time CPI data for food-at-home prices, not a static number from years ago. That makes the Thrifty Food Plan genuinely useful as a floor: it represents what a nutritionally complete diet costs when every dollar is being stretched, cooked from scratch, with no convenience markup.

If your own grocery spending sits noticeably above the Thrifty Plan figure for your exact household composition (after applying the size adjustment), that gap is where a frugal grocery experiment has real room to work, whether through meal planning, buying lower-cost protein sources, or simply tracking where convenience foods are adding cost without adding nutrition.

Key takeaways

  • The USDA publishes four official food plans monthly; the Thrifty Food Plan is the cheapest level and the basis for SNAP benefit calculations.
  • As of August 2026, the official Thrifty Food Plan reference family of four (two adults, two school-age children) cost $1,023.70 a month, entirely home-cooked.
  • Smaller or larger households use the USDA's official size-adjustment percentages, from adding 20% for a one-person household to subtracting 10% for seven or more people.
  • The Thrifty Plan is a floor, not a target: the Low-Cost, Moderate-Cost, and Liberal plans show what the same nutritional adequacy costs with more flexibility.
  • Comparing your real grocery spending against your household's adjusted Thrifty Plan figure gives a grounded starting point for a budgeting experiment, instead of a guessed round number.

The USDA's numbers update monthly, so the specific dollar figures above will drift with food inflation. The age-sex categories and the household-size adjustment percentages are the more durable part of this reference, and the government source is the place to check current figures before setting a new budget.
